\section{Why an area is nearly blind to the split between $\ke$ and $\ka$}
\label{sec:theory}

Three facts about the gut explain the empirical results of Section~\ref{sec:results}; Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives
the proofs. Let the gut be Eq.~\eqref{eq:gut} with initial contents $Q_1(0)=D_0$ and $Q_2(0)=Q_0$. Let
$R_a(t)=\ka Q_2(t)$ be the rate at which glucose enters the plasma. The rates $\ke$ and $\ka$ enter the remaining
equations only through $R_a$.

\begin{proposition}[Exchange symmetry]
\label{prop:exchange}
If the intestine is empty at the start ($Q_0=0$), whatever the stomach content $D_0$, then the Laplace
transform of $R_a$ is
\begin{equation}
\hat R_a(s)=\frac{\ke\,\ka\,\bigl(D_0+\hat m(s)\bigr)}{(s+\ke)(s+\ka)},
\end{equation}
which is symmetric in $(\ke,\ka)$. Hence exchanging the two rates leaves $R_a$, the trajectory of
$(G,I,X)$ and every observable computed from it unchanged.
\end{proposition}

The symmetry is not new in spirit. Oral pharmacokinetics has a related ambiguity: flip-flop occurs when absorption is
slower than elimination, and the terminal slope after extravascular dosing then reflects absorption rather than
disposition \citep{boxenbaum1998flipflop,yanez2011flipflop}. What the paper adds is a measurement of what stays
identifiable when such a model is fitted per person to free-living data, with a replica and a synthetic study to say how
much of the failure belongs to the target.

A discrete symmetry does not make the information matrix singular at a point with $\ke\ne\ka$, and the sensitivity
matrix indeed has full generic rank (Section~\ref{sec:structural}). It makes the likelihood a function of the unordered
pair $\{\ke,\ka\}$. A profile over $\ke$ with $\ka$ re-optimized then has two equal minima, unless the swapped point
lies outside the box. The natural coordinates are the symmetric functions
\begin{equation}
\tau_1=\frac1{\ke}+\frac1{\ka},\qquad p=\frac1{\ke\ka},
\end{equation}
the mean transit time through the gut and its companion. They determine the pair, and in them the real-pole condition
reads $p\le\tau_1^2/4$, with equality at $\ke=\ka$.

\begin{proposition}[Breaking by residual intestinal content]
\label{prop:breaking}
If $Q_0>0$, the appearance rates under the two orderings differ by
\begin{equation}
R_a^{(\ke,\ka)}(t)-R_a^{(\ka,\ke)}(t)=Q_0\bigl(\ka e^{-\ka t}-\ke e^{-\ke t}\bigr),
\end{equation}
which has zero integral. The exchange then changes the timing of glucose entry, linearly in $Q_0$, and
not the total.
\end{proposition}

The simulator, and every analysis here, starts each meal with an empty intestine. Free-living meals are not isolated, and
a snack or a long previous meal leaves a residue. Proposition~\ref{prop:breaking} says the symmetry is then partly
broken, and by how much. The largest glucose change under the exchange grows linearly in the residual content, from
$\resGutSweepMaxHalf$\,mg\,dL$^{-1}$ at one half of one percent of the meal to $\resGutSweepMaxFive$ at five percent and
$\resGutSweepMaxTen$ at ten percent (Appendix, Figure~\ref{fig:gutsweep}). With an empty gut, exchanging the rates changes
the simulated glucose by at most $\resGutSweepMaxZero$\,mg\,dL$^{-1}$, the solver tolerance.

\begin{proposition}[What the moments of the gut input determine]
\label{prop:moments}
For a unit instantaneous meal and an empty intestine, the density $\phi$ of the gut appearance has
$m_0=1$, $m_1=\tau_1$ and $m_2=2\tau_1^2-2p$. Hence $(m_1,m_2)$ determine the unordered pair
$\{\ke,\ka\}$, while $m_1$ alone determines only $\tau_1$. For the linearized model with impulse response
$h$ from $R_a$ to glucose and $H(t)=\int_0^t h$,
\begin{equation}
\int_0^T y\,dt=K\!\int_0^T R_a(s)\,ds-\int_0^T R_a(s)\,\bigl(K-H(T-s)\bigr)\,ds,
\qquad K=\lim_{t\to\infty}H(t),
\end{equation}
so at a finite window the area depends on the rates through the mass delivered inside the window, the first term, and
through the part of the response that falls beyond its end, the second. As $T\to\infty$ the first term tends to the
total mass, which is the same for every pair of rates, and the second vanishes.
\end{proposition}

Moment theory for absorption data is classical in pharmacokinetics, where the mean absorption time is estimated from
plasma-concentration moments \citep{cutler1978theory,yamaoka1978statistical,riegelman1980application}; we restate it for the
two-compartment cascade so that the argument is self-contained, and claim no novelty. Proposition~\ref{prop:moments} orders the
observables. An area over a long window sees the total mass delivered, the zeroth moment, which is the same for every pair of
rates; at a finite window it sees timing only through the share of the mass delivered by the window's end and through what leaks
past it. The centroid adds the first moment, and so $\tau_1$. Only the shape of the trace, with its rise, peak and decay, can
carry the second moment and hence $p$. The sensitivity of the area to timing, relative to its sensitivity to $\SI$, falls with the
window (Figure~\ref{fig:window}), and the weakest Fisher direction is the $\tau_1$-preserving direction, which is the exchange
direction to first order (Figure~\ref{fig:symmetry}).

The three propositions describe a two-compartment gut cascade, not this particular simulator: any linear series cascade with an
empty downstream compartment has a transfer function that is symmetric in its rate constants. A model with a different gut
(Section~\ref{sec:dm}) does not inherit an exact symmetry, and what survives there is an empirical question.
