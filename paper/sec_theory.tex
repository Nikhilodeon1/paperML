\section{What the gut moments predict}
\label{sec:theory}

This section restates textbook structure so that the results of Section~\ref{sec:results} can be read against it; proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
In Eq.~\eqref{eq:gut} with initial contents $Q_1(0)=D_0$ and $Q_2(0)=Q_0$, let $R_a(t)=\ka Q_2(t)$ be the rate at which glucose appears in plasma.
The rates enter Eqs.~\eqref{eq:glucose}--\eqref{eq:action} only through $R_a$.

\begin{proposition}[Moments of the gut input]
\label{prop:moments}
For a unit instantaneous meal into an empty intestine ($Q_0=0$), the appearance density has mean $\tau_1=1/\ke+1/\ka$ and second moment $2\tau_1^2-2p$, with $p=1/(\ke\ka)$ and $p\le\tau_1^2/4$ (equality at $\ke=\ka$).
Hence the first two moments fix the unordered pair $\{\ke,\ka\}$, and the first moment alone fixes only $\tau_1$.
In the linearized glucose system, the iAUC over a window $T$ depends on the rates only through the mass delivered before $T$ and the tail of the response past $T$; as $T\to\infty$ it depends on neither.
\end{proposition}

Moment methods for absorption are classical in pharmacokinetics \citep{cutler1978theory,yamaoka1978statistical,riegelman1980application}.
Their consequence here is an ordering of the targets: a long-window area sees the delivered mass, the centroid adds the first moment and so $\tau_1$, and only the shape of the trace can carry the second moment and so $p$.

\begin{proposition}[Exchange symmetry of an empty gut]
\label{prop:exchange}
If the intestine starts empty, the Laplace transform of $R_a$ is $\ke\ka\,(D_0+\hat m(s))/((s+\ke)(s+\ka))$, which is symmetric in $(\ke,\ka)$.
Exchanging the two rates therefore leaves $R_a$, the trajectory of $(G,I,X)$ and every observable unchanged.
\end{proposition}

This is the flip-flop ambiguity of oral pharmacokinetics \citep{boxenbaum1998flipflop,yanez2011flipflop} in the gut cascade.
It does not make the information matrix singular at a point with $\ke\ne\ka$; it makes the likelihood a function of the unordered pair, so a profile over $\ke$ has two equal minima unless the swapped point lies outside the box.
The boxes are asymmetric, so the swap partner of an upper-pinned estimate often lies outside, and the symmetry cannot by itself explain flat profiles.

\begin{proposition}[Breaking by residual intestinal content]
\label{prop:breaking}
If $Q_0>0$, the two orderings differ by $Q_0\bigl(\ka e^{-\ka t}-\ke e^{-\ke t}\bigr)$ in $R_a(t)$, which integrates to zero: residual content changes the timing of appearance, linearly in $Q_0$, and not the total mass.
\end{proposition}

Numerically, the largest glucose change under exchange grows from $\resGutSweepMaxHalf$\,mg\,dL$^{-1}$ at $0.5\%$ residual content to $\resGutSweepMaxFive$ at $5\%$ and $\resGutSweepMaxTen$ at $10\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:gutsweep} in Appendix~\ref{app:results}), against $\resGutSweepMaxZero$ for an empty gut.

These statements describe any linear two-compartment cascade, and they make three predictions that the data can contradict: the centroid should bound $\tau_1$, a gut model without the symmetry should be recoverable, and a known residue should make the rates identifiable.
Section~\ref{sec:results} finds that none of the three holds in full, which points to the information in the data, and not to the symmetry, as the main limit.
