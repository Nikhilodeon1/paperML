\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A glucose digital twin can report that a user has low insulin sensitivity or slow gastric emptying.
Such a report treats parameters of a mechanistic simulator, fitted to continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) traces and meal logs, as measurements of that person.
A natural check on a fitted twin is whether it predicts meals it has not seen.
That check constrains whatever combination of parameters drives the prediction.
It need not constrain each parameter, because an optimizer returns a value for every parameter whether or not the data determine it.

Identifiability analysis separates what the data determine from what the estimator supplies \citep[see][for reviews]{cobelli1980parameter,miao2011identifiability,villaverde2016structural}.
The ambiguities in question have long histories: flip-flop kinetics in oral absorption \citep{boxenbaum1998flipflop,yanez2011flipflop} and zero estimates of insulin sensitivity in minimal-model fits \citep{pillonetto2002minimal}.
The closest recent study we know calibrates personalized models on oral glucose tolerance tests with CGM and plasma insulin, and reports better practical identifiability with CGM than with venous glucose \citep{erdos2024leveraging}.
Our setting differs in what is observed and in how the answer is checked.
The meals are free-living and self-logged, there is no insulin measurement, and every identifiability claim is tested against simulations whose true parameters we know.

We study a Bergman-type simulator, implemented in JAX with differentiable ODE solvers \citep{bradbury2018jax,kidger2021diffrax}, with three personalized parameters: insulin sensitivity $\SI$, gastric emptying $\ke$, and carbohydrate absorption $\ka$.
We ask which of them are determined by two targets, the incremental area under the glucose curve (iAUC) and the full postprandial trace, and whether held-out prediction reveals the ones that are not.
We keep apart two things that are easy to merge.
A profile-likelihood interval is \emph{bounded} when the likelihood closes it inside the search range.
It is \emph{calibrated} when intervals of that kind contain the true value as often as they claim to, which only data with known truth can show.
Neither one establishes that a parameter fitted to real CGM data measures physiology.

Our contributions are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The area does not determine the gut rates, and held-out area error does not show it.} No interval for $\ke$ or $\ka$ is bounded at windows from $60$ to $180$\,min or in search ranges up to four times the primary one. On simulated subjects, fits with both rates pinned to wrong extremes and $\SI$ refitted stay within a factor of \resNegZeroFastRefitIauc{} of the error of the true model. Of the other held-out targets we tried, only the time of the glucose peak separates them from the fitted twin, and only in part (Sections~\ref{sec:timing} and~\ref{sec:prediction}).
\item \textbf{The full trace can recover two rate combinations, and carbohydrate logging error takes that away.} With exact inputs, intervals for $\tau_1=1/\ke+1/\ka$ and $p=1/(\ke\ka)$ are close to nominal. Logging error moves the estimates without widening the intervals. Because the same shift can close intervals or open them, a count of bounded intervals does not indicate which data are better (Section~\ref{sec:more}).
\item \textbf{A different gut model moves the target itself.} When a Dalla Man model generates the data, the value of $\tau_1$ that the Bergman model converges to without noise differs from the generator's mean appearance time by a median of \resPtBias{}\,min. The order of subjects is largely preserved nonetheless (Section~\ref{sec:dm}).
\end{enumerate}
