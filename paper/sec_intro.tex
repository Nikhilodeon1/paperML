\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A glucose digital twin can tell a person that their insulin sensitivity is low or that their stomach empties
slowly. These statements are parameters of a simulator fitted to that person's continuous glucose monitoring
(CGM) record and meal log, and the usual evidence that they deserve trust is that the fitted simulator predicts
held-out data. Prediction can be good while the parameters are not determined. If two parameters enter the
observation only through a combination, the data fix the combination and leave the split open; the optimizer
returns a split anyway, and the split reflects its starting point and the parameter range.

Identifiability analysis separates what the data determine from what the fitting procedure supplies
\citep[see][for reviews]{cobelli1980parameter,miao2011identifiability,villaverde2016structural}. Related
ambiguities are old: flip-flop kinetics in oral pharmacokinetics \citep{boxenbaum1998flipflop,yanez2011flipflop}, and
insulin sensitivities of zero returned by least-squares fits of the minimal model \citep{pillonetto2002minimal}.
Personalised glucose--insulin models calibrated on an oral glucose tolerance test with CGM and plasma insulin have been
examined for practical identifiability \citep{erdos2024leveraging}. We study the case in which twins are deployed:
meals that people log themselves, no plasma insulin, and a simulator that is differentiable end to end
\citep{kidger2021diffrax,bradbury2018jax}, so that Fisher information and profile likelihoods are available for every
person.

We use a Bergman-type simulator with a two-compartment gut and two targets. The first is the baseline-subtracted
incremental area under the glucose curve (iAUC) of a meal, the summary that postprandial prediction work
standardly uses \citep{zeevi2015personalized,berry2020human}. The second is the full postprandial trace, to which
mechanistic twins are usually calibrated \citep{erdos2024leveraging,hoang2025t1dsbi} and which carries far more
timing information. Three questions follow. Which of insulin sensitivity $\SI$, gastric emptying rate $\ke$ and
carbohydrate absorption rate $\ka$ do the data determine? Is the answer a property of the target, the optimizer,
the noise or a wrong model? Would held-out prediction have told us? We answer with Fisher information,
profile-likelihood intervals with fixed definitions of bounded, one-sided and flat, a generic-rank test and
symbolic structural analysis. Two instruments that real data cannot supply carry the weight: a \emph{replica}, in
which the model is true and the noise and the carbohydrate-logging error are the subject's own, and a synthetic
study with randomly drawn truths. We replicate on three cohorts, repeat the prediction comparison on a second
cohort and run the iAUC analysis on a second gut model.

Our contributions are five.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We find that the iAUC carries little information about the timing of glucose entry. Moments of the gut input explain why
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:moments}), and the Fisher information of three parameters has one direction that is
  essentially null. That direction is the exchange of the two gut rates, which is an exact symmetry of the empty-gut
  cascade (Proposition~\ref{prop:exchange}). It is the weakest direction in every subject of three cohorts, and no
  profile interval for either rate is bounded (Sections~\ref{sec:theory} and~\ref{sec:timing}).
\item We show that the interval for insulin sensitivity is bounded for \resProfileIaucSIInteriorBoundedOne{} of \resProfileIaucSIInteriorNOne{}
  subjects with an interior estimate (\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOne\%, Wilson interval
  \resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOneLow\ to \resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOneHigh\%) at the primary parameter box, and for
  \resSeedsSIBounded\% when the model is exactly true. The figure depends on the box width, and on one replica seed
  cutting the noise to a quarter raises it from \resNoiseOneIaucSI\% to \resNoiseQuarterIaucSI\%
  (Section~\ref{sec:si}).
\item We show that held-out iAUC prediction does not reveal either failure. Gradient fitting and grid search differ by
  \resDiffGradThreeVsGridThree{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$\,min, and three parameters match insulin sensitivity alone within a
  stated margin, in two cohorts. On replicas with random truths the held-out error of the fit is \resPvpRandomZeroRatio{}
  (seed zero) and \resPvpRandomOneRatio{} (seed one) times that of the true model, while the median root mean squared log error of
  the two rates is \resPvpRandomZeroTiming{} and \resPvpRandomOneTiming{}. Held-out peak-time and trace error, which the fits
  were not trained on, rise with the timing error (Section~\ref{sec:prediction}).
\item We show that the sum $\tau_1=1/\ke+1/\ka$ and product $p=1/(\ke\ka)$ of the reciprocal rates, which the exchange symmetry
  leaves free, look recoverable from the full trace on real data but are not shown to be: a true-model replica does not
  reproduce the real-data fractions, and a carbohydrate logging error makes its intervals more often bounded and less often
  right. They are bounded and calibrated only in a replica with exact carbohydrate and a quarter of the noise
  (Section~\ref{sec:more}).
\item We show that a design that breaks the exchange symmetry, a known residual gut content, raises the share of bounded
  gut-rate intervals in proportion to the residue but does not make the rates identifiable at a tenth of the meal
  (Section~\ref{sec:more}). A second gut model leaves its gut parameters unbounded as well (Section~\ref{sec:dm}).
\end{enumerate}

The claims hold for one model family, one target pair and three cohorts; Section~\ref{sec:limits} says where they stop.
