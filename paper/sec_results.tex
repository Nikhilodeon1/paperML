\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Proportions carry $95\%$ Wilson intervals and paired differences carry $95\%$ subject-level BCa bootstrap intervals.
Everything below is exploratory except the ten hypotheses of Table~\ref{tab:plan}. Appendix~\ref{app:results} holds the supporting tables.

\subsection{The gut rates are not bounded under iAUC}
\label{sec:timing}\label{sec:structural}

At the primary box and a $180$\,min window, the Fisher information of $(\log\SI,\log\ke,\log\ka)$ has median eigenvalues \resEigMedianOne, \resEigMedianTwo, and \resEigMedianThree: one direction is essentially null and a second is weak.
The weakest eigenvector of the timing block lies along the exchange tangent $(1/\ka,-1/\ke)$ with a median $|\cos|$ of \resCosineIaucOne, and every subject of all three cohorts exceeds $0.8$ (Figure~\ref{fig:symmetry}b).
The alignment is not specific to iAUC, since it is also found under the full trace (\resCosineTraceOne); what iAUC changes is the size of the smallest eigenvalue.
Recomputing the spectrum at a solver tolerance of $10^{-8}$ for all \resTolN{} subjects changes the eigenvalues by a median of \resTolMedianRelChange\% (largest change \resTolMaxRelChange\%) and keeps the cosine above \resTolMinCosine.

These iAUC profiles use the earlier protocol of Appendix~\ref{app:spec}, with a $15$-point grid and a fixed-step inner solver.
Table~\ref{tab:box} counts bounded profile intervals for the three parameters under iAUC at three box widths.
No interval for $\ke$ or $\ka$ is bounded at any width.
At the primary box only \resBoxOneKeInt{} and \resBoxOneKaInt{} subjects have an interior estimate, so that row cannot support a fraction; at twice the width the counts are \resBoxTwoKeInt{} and \resBoxTwoKaInt, and at four times \resBoxFourKeInt{} and \resBoxFourKaInt.
A box this wide is the more informative test, because a narrow box forces most estimates onto a wall (\resPinnedFracKeOne\% of $\ke$ and \resPinnedFracKaOne\% of $\ka$ estimates at $1\times$, against \resPinnedFracKeTwo\% and \resPinnedFracKaTwo\% at $2\times$).
Shortening the window moves estimates off the wall (\resWinProfSixtyKeInt{} and \resWinProfSixtyKaInt{} interior subjects at $60$\,min) without producing a bounded interval at $60$, $90$, $120$ or $180$\,min (Appendix~\ref{apx:more}).
The Shanghai and standardized-meal cohorts also have no bounded interval, but with only \resRepShanghaiOneInteriorKe{} and \resRepShanghaiOneInteriorKa{} interior subjects in one and \resRepHallOneInteriorKe{} and \resRepHallOneInteriorKa{} in the other they add little.

\subsection{Insulin sensitivity is bounded for a minority, and only conditionally on fixed constants}
\label{sec:si}

Under the iAUC at the primary box, $\SI$ is bounded for \resProfileIaucSIInteriorBoundedOne{} of the \resProfileIaucSIInteriorNOne{} subjects with an interior estimate (\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOne\%, Wilson interval \resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOneLow--\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOneHigh\%), which is \resRealIaucSIBoundedAll{} of \resRealIaucN{} subjects overall.
The count depends on the prior range: it is \resBoxTwoSIBdAllCount{} of \resBoxTwoSIInt{} interior subjects at $2\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:box}), because a wider box lets more profiles close while also changing which subjects are interior.
On the true-model replica, with the subject's own noise and a $25\%$ carbohydrate error, the interval is bounded for \resSeedsSIBounded\% of interior subjects pooled over five seeds (\resSeedsSIBoundedCount{} of \resSeedsSIInterior{}, subject-clustered interval \resClustSILow--\resClustSIHigh\%) and never for $\ke$ or $\ka$.
With true values drawn at random through the box the bounded share falls with the true $\SI$ (\resSynthTercileSILow\%, \resSynthTercileSIMid\% and \resSynthTercileSIHigh\% across terciles of $90$ subjects), because the response saturates (Figure~\ref{fig:replica}).
Halving or quartering the replica noise raises the share on seed $0$ from \resNoiseOneIaucSI\% to \resNoiseHalfIaucSI\% and \resNoiseQuarterIaucSI\%.
The estimate is also biased downward on the unperturbed replica (median signed log error \resPertBaseBias), and the bias is the same without carbohydrate error (\resExactSIBias, with \resExactSIPinned{} of \resExactSIN{} estimates on a bound), so the logging error does not cause it.

The fixed constants matter in a way the interval cannot show.
Insulin secretion $\gamma$ and the action gain $p_3$ enter the glucose equations only through the product $\gamma\,p_3\,\SI$, so $\hat\SI$ estimates that product and not an isolated sensitivity; multiplying either constant by $0.7$ in the replica truth leaves the interval no less often bounded (\resPertGammaDownSIBounded\% against \resPertBaseSIBounded\%) while the interval covers the truth for only \resPertGammaDownSICover\% of subjects.
Perturbing $p_1$, $p_2$ and $n$ in the generator and not in the fit would test the remaining constants; we did not run it (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Held-out iAUC prediction does not reveal the unidentified rates}
\label{sec:prediction}

On the real data the fitted twin is a modest predictor.
Across $5\times5$-fold cross-validation on CGMacros its mean iAUC error is \resMaeGradThree{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$\,min, \resDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMean{} (\resDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMeanLow{} to \resDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMeanHigh) below that of a subject's own mean (\resMaePersonalMean), a gain of about \resGainPct\%.
It does worse than the personal mean on the peak time (\resPeakGradThree{} against \resPeakPersonalMean{} min) and on the trace (\resTraceRmseGradThree{} against \resTraceRmsePersonalMean{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$), and on Shanghai its interval against the personal mean includes zero (Appendix~\ref{apx:prediction}).
So held-out accuracy does not make the real twin look good, and the real-data comparison cannot tell what the rates add.
The replica can: it asks whether a model that predicts as well as the true model still leaves the rates unidentified.

The timing parameters do add a small, real gain on real data.
Fitting $\ke$ and $\ka$ as well as $\SI$ lowers the iAUC error by \resDiffGradThreeVsGradOne{} (\resDiffGradThreeVsGradOneLow{} to \resDiffGradThreeVsGradOneHigh) on CGMacros and by \resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGradOne{} on Shanghai, and both intervals exclude zero.
The $150$ mg\,dL$^{-1}$\,min equivalence margin is \resMarginShareGain\% of the whole gain over the personal mean, so passing it, as the fit does, says little; we read the differences themselves.
Gradient fitting and exhaustive grid search of the same loss differ by \resDiffGradThreeVsGridThree{} (\resDiffGradThreeVsGridThreeLow{} to \resDiffGradThreeVsGridThreeHigh).
Fitting the rates from the iAUC makes the held-out peak time and trace worse than fixing them at population values (peak \resPeakGradThree{} against \resPeakGradOne{} min; trace \resTraceRmseGradThree{} against \resTraceRmseGradOne{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$).

\paragraph{The true-parameter reference and a negative control.}
On a replica the held-out error of the model with the true parameters is known, and we use it as a reference. It is not a lower bound: under logging error a fit can absorb part of the error through $\SI$ and predict better than the true parameters do.
On the two random-truth replicas, the three-parameter fit has a median held-out iAUC error of \resPvpRandomZeroRatio{} times the reference (seed $0$) and \resPvpRandomOneRatio{} times (seed $1$), while the median root-mean-square log error of $\ke$ and $\ka$ is \resPvpRandomZeroTiming{} and \resPvpRandomOneTiming.
The correlation across subjects between the ratio and the timing error is weak, and its interval straddles zero (Table~\ref{tab:floor}).
To see how much timing error the iAUC can hide, we pinned $(\ke,\ka)$ to the slowest and fastest corners of the box and refitted $\SI$ on each training fold.
On seed $0$ the median iAUC ratio to the reference is \resNegZeroSlowRefitIauc{} for the slow corner and \resNegZeroFastRefitIauc{} for the fast one, compared with \resPvpRandomZeroRatio{} for the fitted rates; on seed $1$ the ratio is \resNegOneSlowRefitIauc{} for the slow corner, and for the fast corner it is \resNegOneFastRefitIauc.
The $95\%$ bootstrap intervals over subjects of the two seed-$0$ medians are \resNegZeroSlowRefitIaucLow--\resNegZeroSlowRefitIaucHigh{} (slow corner) and \resNegZeroFastRefitIaucLow--\resNegZeroFastRefitIaucHigh{} (fast corner).
Of the two other held-out targets, only the peak time discriminates, and only partly: its ratio is \resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeRatio{} for the fit, \resNegZeroSlowRefitPeak{} for the slow corner and \resNegZeroFastRefitPeak{} for the fast one, and for the fit it rises with the timing error (Spearman $\rho$ \resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeRho).
The median trace ratio is about \resFloorTraceRandomZeroThreeRatio{} for the fit and for both corners, so it does not tell the wrong corners from the fit.
Across the subjects of the fit, however, the trace ratio rises with the timing error more clearly than the peak-time ratio does (Table~\ref{tab:floor}).
Both targets therefore carry information on replicas. None of these ratios can be computed on real data, where the true model's error is unknown.

\subsection{A second gut model, and a structural misspecification control}
\label{sec:dm}

The Dalla Man meal model \citep{dallaman2007} has a nonlinear, three-compartment gut and no exact exchange symmetry.
Fitted to the same iAUC records, its Fisher information has median effective rank \resDmRankMedian{} of six parameters, no interval for $k_{abs}$, $k_{max}$ or $k_{min}$ is bounded, and the estimates sit on a bound for \resDmPinnedKabs, \resDmPinnedKmax{} and \resDmPinnedKmin{} of \resDmN{} subjects.
A gut without the symmetry is therefore not recovered either, which supports the reading that the iAUC lacks the information.

The same model serves as a generator.
We simulated traces from it for the \resMisspecN{} CGMacros meal schedules with exact carbohydrates, sampled Dalla Man parameters (one distribution; median mean appearance time $\tau_{1,\mathrm{DM}}$ of \resMisspecTrueTauMed{}\,min), matched subject weights and AR($1$) CGM noise, and fitted the Bergman-type simulator.
The Bergman intervals for $\tau_1$ are bounded in \resMisspecTauBd{} of \resMisspecN{} subjects and cover $\tau_{1,\mathrm{DM}}$ in \resMisspecCovDm\%.
That is close to definitional, because Bergman's $\tau_1$ is not the Dalla Man appearance time: it absorbs the sensor lag, the fixed insulin-action constants and the gut structure the model lacks.
Under misspecification a maximum-likelihood fit converges to a pseudo-true value, which we obtained by fitting the Bergman model to the noise-free Dalla Man output.
That value has a median of \resPtMedianPseudo{}\,min against \resPtMedianDm{}\,min for $\tau_{1,\mathrm{DM}}$, a median per-subject difference, the model bias, of \resPtBias{}\,min (\resPtBiasTd{}\,min if the sensor lag is added), and all \resPtTied{} pseudo-true fits sit at equal rates $\ke=\ka$.
The estimator itself lands within \resPtAbsGap{}\,min of its pseudo-true value, but the intervals are narrow (median log width \resPtWidth) and cover it in \resPtCoversPseudo{} of the \resPtBounded{} bounded cases.
So there are two shortfalls: the model biases the target by tens of minutes, and in this run the intervals also cover the estimator's own target well below the nominal rate.
Subject order is preserved (Spearman $\rho$ of \resPtRhoEstPseudo{} between estimate and pseudo-true value, \resMisspecRhoDm{} against $\tau_{1,\mathrm{DM}}$), so $\hat\tau_1$ can rank subjects when it cannot measure timing.
These intervals come from the exact-domain profiles of the earlier protocol (a $15$-point grid), so the coverage of the pseudo-true value is probably understated; a Bergman variant with sensor lag and a free $p_2$, a Dalla Man-on-Dalla-Man replica and a second generator distribution were not run.
