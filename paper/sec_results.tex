\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Hypotheses H1 to H10 were fixed in version control before the analyses. H11 and H12 were declared before theirs, and
H13 to H34 after earlier results had been seen; those are marked post hoc. Fractions carry Wilson intervals and paired
differences carry subject-level bootstrap intervals. Appendix~\ref{app:results} gives the tables and figures omitted
here, and Appendix~\ref{app:scorecard} the status of every hypothesis.

\subsection{An iAUC carries little information about $\ke$ and $\ka$}
\label{sec:timing}\label{sec:structural}

\paragraph{What the area carries.}
At the primary box the Fisher information of $(\log\SI,\log\ke,\log\ka)$ under iAUC has median eigenvalues
\resEigMedianOne, \resEigMedianTwo{} and \resEigMedianThree. One direction is essentially null and a second is weak,
so the area carries between one and two effective dimensions of the three parameters. The coordinates in which the
exchange leaves the model unchanged, $\tau_1=1/\ke+1/\ka$ and $p=1/(\ke\ka)$, are not bounded either: under iAUC the
interval for $\tau_1$ is bounded in \resCoordsBoundedTauIauc\% and that for $p$ in \resCoordsBoundedPIauc\% of subjects
interior for them. The exchange of the two rates is therefore not the whole of the failure. It is the exact
symmetry that Proposition~\ref{prop:exchange} proves in the empty-gut model, and the rest of this section shows that it
is also the weakest direction of the information matrix.

\paragraph{Structure.}
Symbolic analysis with StructuralIdentifiability.jl (Appendix~\ref{app:structural}) finds the rates globally
identifiable when the intestinal content is a known generic value, and only locally identifiable when it is
unconstrained. In canonical coordinates the sum $\ke+\ka$ and the product $\ke\ka$ are globally identifiable
(Table~\ref{tab:structural}). The plan said to drop the exchange framing if the rates were globally identifiable, and
the planned formulation triggers that rule. H1 is therefore reported as formulation-dependent, not as met. We keep the
framing because the empty-gut model that the simulator runs is exactly symmetric (Proposition~\ref{prop:exchange}):
exchanging the rates changes simulated glucose by at most $\resGutSweepMaxZero$\,mg\,dL$^{-1}$. The stacked
sensitivity matrix has generic rank three in \resRankFullIauc{} of \resRankDrawsIauc{} random draws for iAUC and in
every draw for the richer observables. Under iAUC the parameters are locally identifiable but ill-conditioned.

\paragraph{Estimates, Fisher information and profiles.}
At the primary box the maximum-likelihood estimates of $\ke$ and $\ka$ sit on a bound in \resPinnedFracKeOne\% and
\resPinnedFracKaOne\% of the \resSubjectsOne{} CGMacros subjects. The optimizer is not the cause. The Adam estimate
exceeds the best of six L-BFGS runs by at most \resOptGapMaxOne{} in negative log-likelihood, below $\delta$.

The weakest eigenvector of the timing block of the Fisher information lies along the exchange direction, with a median
absolute cosine of \resCosineIaucOne{} under iAUC (H4, planned, \resVerdictHFour). It does so in every subject of all
three cohorts (Figure~\ref{fig:symmetry}b; H15, post hoc, \resVerdictHFifteen). No profile interval for $\ke$ or $\ka$
is bounded under iAUC: \resProfileIaucKeOne\% and \resProfileIaucKaOne\% of subjects at the primary box, with upper
Wilson limits of \resProfileIaucKeOneHigh\% and \resProfileIaucKaOneHigh\%. The same holds at half and twice the box and
with the grid extended to four times the upper bound (timing clause of H5, planned, met). The Shanghai cohort
(\resRepShanghaiOneN{} subjects) and the cohort with standardized meals (\resRepHallOneN{} subjects) agree. Most of
their estimates sit on a bound at the primary box, so the replication rests on the Fisher alignment and the flat
profiles. Part of the pinning is an area deficit. Subjects whose rates sit on the upper bound have much larger
responses and are under-predicted by \resBiasSignedKe{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$\,min on average, so the model cannot deliver
their area at any admissible timing. The eigenvalues are not an artifact of the solver tolerance. In the first
\resTolSubjects{} subjects, a relative tolerance of $10^{-8}$ in place of $10^{-4}$ changes the smallest eigenvalue by
a median of \resTolMedianRel\%, the absolute cosine of the weak direction with the exchange direction stays at or above
\resTolMinCos, and none of \resStepsProfiles{} profile classes changes when the inner optimizer takes $500$ steps in
place of $100$ (\resStepsChanged{} changed). The largest change of an eigenvalue, \resTolMaxRel\%, belongs to a
subject whose smallest eigenvalue is at numerical zero (Appendix~\ref{apx:phase13}).

\subsection{$\SI$ is recoverable for some subjects and not others, and noise explains only part of why}
\label{sec:si}

\paragraph{Real data.}
The plan required the $\SI$ interval to be bounded in at least $80\%$ of subjects interior for $\SI$ (H5, planned).
It is bounded in \resProfileIaucSIInteriorBoundedOne{} of \resProfileIaucSIInteriorNOne{}
(\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOne\%, Wilson interval \resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOneLow\ to
\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracOneHigh\%) at the primary box, in \resProfileIaucSIInteriorBoundedHalf{} of
\resProfileIaucSIInteriorNHalf{} (\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracHalf\%, \resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracHalfLow\ to
\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracHalfHigh\%) at half the box and in \resProfileIaucSIInteriorBoundedTwo{} of
\resProfileIaucSIInteriorNTwo{} (\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracTwo\%, \resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracTwoLow\ to
\resProfileIaucSIInteriorFracTwoHigh\%) at twice the box. The clause fails at every width (\resVerdictHFive).
A wider box lets more profiles close, but it also changes which subjects count as interior, so the three
denominators differ. The fractions describe what the data support within a stated range, not a property of the
subject. Most intervals are one-sided, and the picture holds at the $90\%$ level (\resLevelNinetySIInteriorOne\%) and
the $99\%$ level (\resLevelNinetyNineSIInteriorOne\%).

\paragraph{When the model is true.}
The replica gives the same answer. The model is exactly true, the noise is the subject's own and the carbohydrate
logging error is $25\%$. On seed zero the interval is bounded in \resReplicaSIBoundedCount{} of
\resReplicaSIInterior{} interior subjects, or \resReplicaSIBounded\% (H13, post hoc, \resVerdictHThirteen). Pooled over
five seeds it is bounded in \resSeedsSIBounded\% (\resSeedsSIBoundedCount{} of \resSeedsSIInterior{}; the seeds reuse
the same subjects and meal schedules, so we resample subjects and not subject--seed pairs, which gives
\resClustSILow\ to \resClustSIHigh\% against \resClustSINaiveLow\ to \resClustSINaiveHigh\% from the Wilson interval
that ignores the clustering), with
\resSeedsRangeLow--\resSeedsRangeHigh\% across seeds and no seed reaching the $50\%$ of the rule (H17, post hoc,
\resVerdictHSeventeen). The pooled bounded intervals are calibrated, with coverage \resSeedsCoverSI\%. The interval for
$\ke$ or $\ka$ is never bounded.

With true parameters drawn across the box (the synthetic study, \resSynthN{} subjects, post hoc), the $\SI$ interval is
bounded for \resSynthTercileSILow\%, \resSynthTercileSIMid\% and \resSynthTercileSIHigh\% of interior subjects in the
lower, middle and upper third of the true value (Figure~\ref{fig:replica}b), with coverage \resSynthCoverSI\%. Again no
interval for $\ke$ or $\ka$ is bounded: the estimates return a rate on a bound even when the truth lies inside the box.

\paragraph{How much is noise.}
The replica noise is estimated from real residuals, which include model misfit, so it is an upper bound on the sensor
noise. Scaling it to one half and one quarter raises the bounded fraction of $\SI$ under iAUC from
\resNoiseOneIaucSI\% to \resNoiseHalfIaucSI\% and \resNoiseQuarterIaucSI\% (seed zero throughout, so the first figure
is the seed-zero \resReplicaSIBounded\% and not the pooled \resSeedsSIBounded\%; H24, post hoc). That is still
below the $80\%$ of the plan. The weakness is partly a property of the noise level and partly of the target.
Carbohydrate logging error does not cause it, since \resExactOneIaucSI\% are bounded with exact carbohydrate. Two
heteroskedastic variants of the likelihood do not bound $\SI$ more often either (\resHeteroRoot\% and \resHeteroProp\%
against \resHeteroBaseline\%; H16, exploratory; Appendix~\ref{apx:si}). A systematic error in the carbohydrate log is
absorbed by $\SI$. Scaling every logged carbohydrate by $0.75$ and $1.33$ moves the median estimate by a log ratio of
\resCarbScaleLowLogRatio{} and \resCarbScaleHighLogRatio{}, close to the \resCarbScaleLowExpected{} and
\resCarbScaleHighExpected{} of exact absorption (H16, exploratory).

\paragraph{A wrong fixed parameter.}
Two parameters of the glucose equations, $\gamma$ (insulin secretion) and $p_3$ (the gain of insulin action), are fixed
at population values in every fit. To see what a wrong value does, we multiplied one of them in the truth of the
standard replica by $1.3$ and by $0.7$ and kept the population value in the fit (Amendment eleven, post hoc). The two
parameters act alike by construction. In the equations, insulin above its basal level is proportional to $\gamma$ and
drives the action state through $p_3\SI$, so the glucose trace depends on the three only through the product
$\gamma\,p_3\,\SI$; scaling $\gamma$ or $p_3$ by a factor is the same as scaling $\SI$ by that factor. The fits agree:
the estimate of $\SI$ is the same in \resPertSameUp{} of \resPertSameUpN{} subjects at $1.3$ and \resPertSameDown{} of
\resPertSameDownN{} at $0.7$, the rest differing only through the optimizer and the box. We therefore treat the two as
one test, a change in the scale of insulin action, and read $\SI$ as that scale. A wrong fixed parameter is absorbed by
it one for one only while the estimate stays inside its box. At $0.7$, \resPertGammaDownPinned{} estimates end on a
bound, against \resPertBasePinned{} on the unperturbed replica, and among the interior estimates the median error moves
by \resPertGammaDownShiftObserved{} where the logarithm of the factor is \resPertGammaDownShiftExpected{}; at $1.3$ the
shift is \resPertGammaUpShiftObserved{} against \resPertGammaUpShiftExpected{}. On the unperturbed replica the nominal
interval for $\SI$ contains the truth for \resPertBaseSICover\% of subjects, with a median signed log error of
\resPertBaseBias{} over all subjects. With the truth's insulin action raised by thirty percent the coverage is
\resPertGammaUpSICover\% and the median signed log error \resPertGammaUpBias{}; lowered by thirty percent the coverage
falls to \resPertGammaDownSICover\%, the bounded intervals contain the truth in \resPertGammaDownSICovbd\% of cases, and
the median signed log error is \resPertGammaDownBias{}. The interval for $\SI$ does not signal the error: the share of
bounded intervals is \resPertGammaDownSIBounded\% against \resPertBaseSIBounded\% on the unperturbed replica.

\subsection{Held-out iAUC prediction does not reveal the failures}
\label{sec:prediction}

Gradient fitting and exhaustive grid search of the same loss differ in held-out iAUC error by
\resDiffGradThreeVsGridThree{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$\,min ($95\%$ interval \resDiffGradThreeVsGridThreeLow{} to
\resDiffGradThreeVsGridThreeHigh) with three parameters, on a mean error of \resMaeGradThree{}. This is CGMacros,
$\resCohortCgmSubjects$ subjects, five repeats of five-fold cross-validation (Table~\ref{tab:prediction}). The $90\%$
intervals lie inside the margins of $150$ and $90$ (H7, planned, \resVerdictHSeven). Three parameters and $\SI$ alone
differ by \resDiffGradThreeVsGradOne{} (\resDiffGradThreeVsGradOneLow{} to \resDiffGradThreeVsGradOneHigh), which is
equivalent at the same margins (H8, planned, \resVerdictHEight). The timing parameters add less to held-out iAUC error
than the margin, although the interval excludes zero: the gain is small and not nil. A full-trace objective does not lower the error; it is \resDiffGradThreeTraceVsGradThree{} worse (H9,
planned, \resVerdictHNine), although it improves the quantities it targets.

The gradient fit beats the personal mean on CGMacros (\resDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMean{},
\resDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMeanLow{} to \resDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMeanHigh, on a personal-mean error of
\resMaePersonalMean), a random forest and a simulation-based-inference estimate. We call these
differences, not equivalences. On Shanghai the interval against the personal mean includes zero
(\resShCvDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMean{}, \resShCvDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMeanLow{} to
\resShCvDiffGradThreeVsPersonalMeanHigh), so the twin is a modest gain over a subject's own average there at best. In the Shanghai cohort (\resShCvSubjects{} subjects) the two comparisons give
\resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGridThree{} (\resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGridThreeLow{} to \resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGridThreeHigh) and
\resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGradOne{} (\resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGradOneLow{} to \resShCvDiffGradThreeVsGradOneHigh), inside the
margin of $150$, which is \resShCvMarginShare\% of the personal-mean error (H20, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwenty). There
the three-parameter fit beats the one-parameter fit by an interval that excludes zero. The timing parameters carry a
small amount of predictive information, and we do not say that they carry none.

Prediction of other quantities points the same way, and more sharply. On CGMacros the three-parameter fit predicts the
time of the peak with an error of \resPeakGradThree{} minutes and the whole trace with a root mean squared error of
\resTraceRmseGradThree{} mg\,dL$^{-1}$, against \resPeakGradOne{} minutes and \resTraceRmseGradOne{} for the fit of $\SI$
alone with the population timing held fixed (Table~\ref{tab:prediction}). Fitting the two timing parameters makes these
held-out predictions worse, which is consistent with fitted values that reflect the optimizer's choice more than the
subject's physiology. Our claim that prediction does not reveal the failures is therefore limited to the target the model
was fitted to, the iAUC. Prediction of the peak time and the trace does register a cost of the fitted timing parameters,
and we recommend reporting such checks beside the target-matched one.

\paragraph{Held-out error against parameter error.}
A replica knows the truth, so the claim can be measured as well as argued. For each subject we compare the
held-out iAUC error of the three-parameter fit with the error of the model at the true parameters on the same meals,
with the same logged carbohydrate (the floor), and with the error of the iAUC estimate in the parameters (Figure~\ref{fig:pvp};
Amendment nine, post hoc). On the replica whose truths are the fitted values (seed zero, \resPvpFitTruthN{} subjects)
the median held-out error of the fit is \resPvpFitTruthMae{} and the median floor is \resPvpFitTruthFloor{}, a median
ratio of \resPvpFitTruthRatio{}, against \resPvpFitTruthMean{} for the personal mean. The timing parameters of this
replica sit near the upper bound, where the estimate also lies, so their median log error of \resPvpFitTruthTiming{} says
little. The test is the two replicas with truths drawn at random across the box (seeds zero and one). There the median
ratio to the floor is \resPvpRandomZeroRatio{} and \resPvpRandomOneRatio{}, while the median root mean squared log error
of $\ke$ and $\ka$ is \resPvpRandomZeroTiming{} and \resPvpRandomOneTiming{}, and that of $\SI$ is
\resPvpRandomZeroSI{} and \resPvpRandomOneSI{}. The fit comes within a few percent of the true model's error with
timing parameters that are far from the truth. Across subjects the ratio is not related to the timing error. The
Spearman correlation is \resPvpFitTruthRhoTimingRatio{} (\resPvpFitTruthRhoTimingRatioLow{} to
\resPvpFitTruthRhoTimingRatioHigh), \resPvpRandomZeroRhoTimingRatio{} (\resPvpRandomZeroRhoTimingRatioLow{} to
\resPvpRandomZeroRhoTimingRatioHigh) and \resPvpRandomOneRhoTimingRatio{} (\resPvpRandomOneRhoTimingRatioLow{} to
\resPvpRandomOneRhoTimingRatioHigh) in the three replicas, with intervals over subjects that include zero. The raw error
does rise with the parameter error on random-truth seed zero ($\rho=\resPvpRandomZeroRhoRmsMae$, \resPvpRandomZeroRhoRmsMaeLow{}
to \resPvpRandomZeroRhoRmsMaeHigh), but not in the other two replicas, and the relation is gone once the error is
divided by the floor, which is what a subject-level scale effect would produce (we did not test that explanation). For $\SI$ the correlation with the ratio is
negative in all three replicas (\resPvpFitTruthRhoSIRatio{}, \resPvpRandomZeroRhoSIRatio{} and
\resPvpRandomOneRhoSIRatio{}), which is not the sign that prediction would see an error in $\SI$ either. This is a
statement about replicas, where the floor is observable; on real data the floor is not.

The same floor can be asked of two targets that the fits were not trained on (Amendment ten, post hoc, from stored
results). On the random-truth replicas the held-out peak-time error of the three-parameter fit is
\resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeFit{} and \resFloorPeakRandomOneThreeFit{} minutes against floors of
\resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeFloor{} and \resFloorPeakRandomOneThreeFloor{}, a ratio of \resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeRatio{}
and \resFloorPeakRandomOneThreeRatio{}, and its held-out trace error is \resFloorTraceRandomZeroThreeRatio{} and
\resFloorTraceRandomOneThreeRatio{} times the floor. Here the ratio does follow the timing error. The Spearman
correlation with the timing error is \resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeRho{} (\resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeRhoLow{} to
\resFloorPeakRandomZeroThreeRhoHigh) and \resFloorPeakRandomOneThreeRho{} (\resFloorPeakRandomOneThreeRhoLow{} to
\resFloorPeakRandomOneThreeRhoHigh) for the peak time, and \resFloorTraceRandomZeroThreeRho{}
(\resFloorTraceRandomZeroThreeRhoLow{} to \resFloorTraceRandomZeroThreeRhoHigh) and \resFloorTraceRandomOneThreeRho{}
(\resFloorTraceRandomOneThreeRhoLow{} to \resFloorTraceRandomOneThreeRhoHigh) for the trace. The fit of $\SI$ alone, with
the timing held at the population values, is closer to the floor (\resFloorPeakRandomZeroOneRatio{} and
\resFloorPeakRandomOneOneRatio{} for the peak time, \resFloorTraceRandomZeroOneRatio{} and
\resFloorTraceRandomOneOneRatio{} for the trace), and its ratio is unrelated to the timing error of the
three-parameter fit. A check on the peak time or the trace would therefore see the failure that the iAUC check hides, and
fitting the timing from the iAUC costs accuracy on those targets in comparison with leaving it at the population values,
which agrees with the real-data comparison above.

\subsection{A combination of the rates is recoverable only with exact inputs and low noise}
\label{sec:more}

The exchange symmetry leaves a combination of the rates free, $\tau_1=1/\ke+1/\ka$ and $p=1/(\ke\ka)$. The full trace
carries far more timing information than the area (Appendix~\ref{apx:more}). Fitting in coordinates
$(\log\SI,\log\tau_1,\log p)$, the interval for $\tau_1$ is bounded in \resCoordsBoundedTauIauc\%,
\resCoordsBoundedTauCentroid\% and \resCoordsBoundedTauTrace\% of subjects interior for it under iAUC, iAUC with the
centroid and the trace. For $p$ the figures are \resCoordsBoundedPIauc\%, \resCoordsBoundedPCentroid\% and
\resCoordsBoundedPTrace\% (H11, planned, \resVerdictHEleven). The controls we ran do not support reading this as
identifiability. The trace fits are not likelihood optima for \resPolishNotWithinTrace\% of subjects (H21, post hoc,
\resVerdictHTwentyOne). The like-for-like comparison uses polished estimates on both sides. On real data the polished
trace fits bound $\tau_1$ in \resPolishedBoundedTauTraceCount{} of \resPolishedBoundedTauTraceN{}
(\resPolishedBoundedTauTrace\%, \resPolishedBoundedTauTraceLow\ to \resPolishedBoundedTauTraceHigh\%) and $p$ in
\resPolishedBoundedPTraceCount{} of \resPolishedBoundedPTraceN{} (\resPolishedBoundedPTrace\%,
\resPolishedBoundedPTraceLow\ to \resPolishedBoundedPTraceHigh\%) of the subjects interior for them. The polish is a global optimum by the check we planned for it: with \resGlobalN{}
real subjects, thirty random starts in place of five leave the polished likelihood within the interval cutoff of
the earlier one for \resGlobalWithinCount{} (\resGlobalWithin\%, Wilson interval \resGlobalWithinLow\ to
\resGlobalWithinHigh\%); the one subject outside differs by \resGlobalMaxGap{} in negative log-likelihood, and the
bounded fractions are unchanged at \resGlobalRealTau\% and \resGlobalRealP\% (H26, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentySix).
The replica gives the same answer. On a true-model
replica they bound $\tau_1$ in \resReplicaCoordsTauTraceCount{} of \resReplicaCoordsTauTraceN{}
(\resReplicaCoordsTauTrace\%, \resReplicaCoordsTauTraceLow\ to \resReplicaCoordsTauTraceHigh\%) and $p$ in
\resReplicaCoordsPTraceCount{} of \resReplicaCoordsPTraceN{} (\resReplicaCoordsPTrace\%,
\resReplicaCoordsPTraceLow\ to \resReplicaCoordsPTraceHigh\%) (H18, post hoc, \resVerdictHEighteen). The Wilson intervals for
$\tau_1$ overlap, so for $\tau_1$ the difference in how often the interval is bounded is not established; for $p$ they
do not overlap. The coverage is the stronger evidence. The bounded replica
intervals contain the truth in only \resReplicaCoordsCoverTauTrace\% and \resReplicaCoordsCoverPTrace\%. A cutoff
calibrated on the replica lowers the real-data fractions to \resRecalRealCalibratedTau\% and
\resRecalRealCalibratedP\% (H23, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentyThree). Reducing the replica noise does not restore
coverage (H24, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentyFour), but removing the carbohydrate logging error does. With exact
carbohydrate the nominal interval covers the truth for \resExactOneCoverTau\% and \resExactOneCoverP\% of subjects (H25,
post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentyFive), the empirical cutoff is \resExactOneCutoffTau{} against the nominal
\resDeltaThreshold, and the trace bounds $\tau_1$ and $p$ in only \resExactOneTraceBoundedTau\% and
\resExactOneTraceBoundedP\%. An input error therefore makes the trace intervals more often bounded and less often
right. Sweeping the coefficient of variation of the logging error on seed zero (polished trace coordinate profiles,
\resPvpFitTruthN{} subjects) shows a dose response. The bounded fractions for $\tau_1$ and $p$ are
\resCvZeroBoundedTau\% and \resCvZeroBoundedP\% at exact carbohydrate, \resCvTenthBoundedTau\% and
\resCvTenthBoundedP\% at $0.1$, \resCvQuarterBoundedTau\% and \resCvQuarterBoundedP\% at $0.25$,
\resCvHalfBoundedTau\% and \resCvHalfBoundedP\% at $0.5$ and \resCvThreeQuarterBoundedTau\% and
\resCvThreeQuarterBoundedP\% at $0.75$, while the share of subjects whose nominal interval contains the truth falls
from \resCvZeroCoverTau\% and \resCvZeroCoverP\% to \resCvThreeQuarterCoverTau\% and \resCvThreeQuarterCoverP\%
(Appendix~\ref{apx:phase13}). The real-data fractions of \resPolishedBoundedTauTrace\% and
\resPolishedBoundedPTrace\% are reached on the replica for $\tau_1$ at a logging error of $0.5$ to $0.75$ and are
above every replica value for $p$. The real-data pattern is therefore what a larger input error than the one we
assumed would produce, and we cannot show that it is the cause: a mechanism outside the replica would do the same.
At the estimated noise the undercoverage of the replica is explained by the input error, as the exact-carbohydrate
replica shows. A second source of undercoverage appears at low noise, and it lies in how the profile is read, not in the
likelihood or the optimizer. With exact carbohydrate and a quarter of the noise, the nominal interval read off the
stored grid contains the truth for only \resInnerQuarterFiveHundredCoverTau\% and
\resInnerQuarterFiveHundredCoverP\% of subjects (H28, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentyEight), and a longer inner run does
not change that: with $500$ inner steps in place of $100$ the share of grid points flagged as unsettled falls from
\resInnerQuarterHundredUnsettled\% to \resInnerQuarterFiveHundredUnsettled\%, no profile changes class in any of the
four settings we reran (\resInnerQuarterChanged{} of \resInnerQuarterChangedOf{} at a quarter of the noise,
\resInnerRealChanged{} of \resInnerRealChangedOf{} on the real data), and the real-data bounded fractions stay at
\resInnerRealFiveHundredBoundedTau\% and \resInnerRealFiveHundredBoundedP\% (H27, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentySeven).
Three further checks locate the source (Amendments eleven and twelve, post hoc). First, the likelihood at the true
parameters is calibrated at this noise: half the likelihood-ratio statistic has median \resCalQuarterMedianLr{} and
ninetieth percentile \resCalQuarterQNinetyLr{}, against \resCalRefMedian{} and \resCalRefQNinety{} for half a chi-square with
three degrees of freedom, and exceeds the cutoff in \resCalQuarterOver\% of subjects. The stored polished estimate is
within the cutoff of the best optimum found from the truth in \resCalQuarterGapWithin{} of \resCalQuarterN{} subjects,
with a largest gap of \resCalQuarterGapMax{} (H30, post hoc, \resVerdictHThirty). Second, computed directly at the true
value, with the other two coordinates solved to convergence, the profile excess is within the cutoff for
\resTruthQuarterTauDirectWithinCount{} of \resTruthQuarterTauDirectN{} subjects for $\tau_1$ and
\resTruthQuarterPDirectWithinCount{} of \resTruthQuarterPDirectN{} for $p$ (H31, post hoc, \resVerdictHThirtyOne),
whether the solve starts at the estimate or at the truth and whether it uses Adam or L-BFGS-B; those choices change the
median excess by \resTruthQuarterTauSolver{} (solver) and \resTruthQuarterTauStart{} (start). The excess read off the
fifteen-point grid has median \resTruthQuarterTauStoredMedian{} for $\tau_1$ and \resTruthQuarterPStoredMedian{} for $p$,
against \resTruthQuarterTauDirectMedian{} and \resTruthQuarterPDirectMedian{} when computed at the true value. The
difference is linear interpolation across a grid that is wide relative to a narrow profile (median
\resTruthQuarterTauInterp{} for $\tau_1$ and \resTruthQuarterPInterp{} for $p$). At the estimated noise the same
comparison gives \resTruthExactTauStoredMedian{} against \resTruthExactTauDirectMedian{} for $\tau_1$, a smaller
overstatement that leaves the coverage near its nominal level. Third, a converged inner solver (L-BFGS-B at every grid point) changes no profile class in any setting
(\resConvRealChanged{} of \resConvRealChangedOf{} on the real data, \resConvQuarterChanged{} of
\resConvQuarterChangedOf{} at a quarter of the noise) and leaves the real-data bounded fractions at
\resConvRealTauBounded\% and \resConvRealPBounded\% (H32, post hoc, \resVerdictHThirtyTwo); and a grid of
twenty-nine points in place of fifteen leaves them there too (\resGridRealTwentyNineTauBounded\% and
\resGridRealTwentyNinePBounded\%; H34, post hoc, \resVerdictHThirtyFour), changing no class on the real data or on the
exact-carbohydrate and carbohydrate-error replicas (\resGridRealChanged{}, \resGridExactChanged{} and
\resGridRepChanged{} of \resGridRealChangedOf{}). Only the narrow profiles of the low-noise replica depend on the grid.
On a twenty-nine-point grid with the converged solver, the nominal interval contains the truth for
\resConvFineTauCover\% and \resConvFinePCover\% of subjects at a quarter of the noise (H33, post hoc,
\resVerdictHThirtyThree), the detector bounds $\tau_1$ and $p$ in \resConvFineTauBounded\% and \resConvFinePBounded\%
of interior subjects, and the bounded intervals contain the truth in \resConvFineTauCovbd\% and \resConvFinePCovbd\%.

This changes the claim we can make about the combination. There is a setting, a true-model replica with exact
carbohydrate and a quarter of the replica noise, in which the trace profiles return intervals for $\tau_1$ and $p$ that
are both often bounded and calibrated, so the detector can identify the combination when the data and the inputs are
good enough. It is not the setting of the real data. At the estimated noise with exact carbohydrate the intervals are
calibrated (\resExactOneCoverTau\% and \resExactOneCoverP\%) and rarely bounded (\resExactOneTraceBoundedTau\% and
\resExactOneTraceBoundedP\%), and with the carbohydrate error of the replica they are more often bounded and less
often right. We do not claim that the combination is recovered on the real data, whose bounded fractions of
\resPolishedBoundedTauTrace\% and \resPolishedBoundedPTrace\% are consistent with a larger input error or a
smaller noise than we assumed and cannot be assigned to either. Appendix~\ref{apx:more} gives the trail.

\paragraph{Breaking the symmetry by design.}
If the exchange symmetry were the only reason the gut rates are not bounded, a design that breaks it should bound
them. We gave every meal of a random-truth replica with exact carbohydrate a known residual intestinal content,
equal in the truth and in the fit so that the model stays exactly true (Amendment eleven, H29, post hoc). At a content
of $10\%$ of the meal, $\ke$ and $\ka$ are bounded in \resResTenKeBoundedCount{} of \resResTenKeBoundedN{} and
\resResTenKaBoundedCount{} of \resResTenKaBoundedN{} interior subjects (\resResTenKeBounded\% and
\resResTenKaBounded\%), and the nominal interval contains the truth for \resResTenKeCover\% and \resResTenKaCover\% of
subjects. The rule asked for at least half bounded and ninety percent covered, and it was not met
(\resVerdictHTwentyNine). The bounded fraction rises with the content, for $\ke$ from \resResZeroKeBounded\% at none
through \resResFiveKeBounded\% and \resResTenKeBounded\% to \resResTwentyKeBounded\% at $20\%$, and for $\ka$ from
\resResZeroKaBounded\% through \resResFiveKaBounded\% and \resResTenKaBounded\% to \resResTwentyKaBounded\%, while the
intervals stay calibrated (coverage \resResTwentyKeCover\% and \resResTwentyKaCover\% at $20\%$). A second replica seed at
$10\%$ gives \resResSeedOneTenKeBounded\% and \resResSeedOneTenKaBounded\%, and a grid of twenty-nine points leaves the
$10\%$ result at \resGridResTenTwentyNineKeBounded\% and \resGridResTenTwentyNineKaBounded\%. So breaking the symmetry
helps in proportion to the residue, but a tenth of the meal does not make the rates identifiable from these data: the
structural result of Proposition~\ref{prop:breaking} is necessary for a bounded interval and, at this noise, not
sufficient. The residue is a design choice for a control and not a model of free-living meals.

\subsection{A second model class}
\label{sec:dm}

The Dalla Man meal model \citep{dallaman2007}, in our implementation, has a three-compartment gut with a nonlinear
emptying law, so it inherits no exact exchange symmetry. We fitted it by maximum likelihood to the same iAUC data of the
same \resDmN{} subjects (H22, post hoc, \resVerdictHTwentyTwo). Its Fisher information has median effective rank
\resDmRankMedian{} of six parameters. The profile interval is bounded for $k_{abs}$, $k_{max}$ and $k_{min}$ in
\resDmBoundedKabs\%, \resDmBoundedKmax\% and \resDmBoundedKmin\% of subjects, and the estimates sit on a bound of their
range in \resDmPinnedKabs, \resDmPinnedKmax{} and \resDmPinnedKmin{} subjects. The claim this supports is narrow. Under
the iAUC target, in two models that differ in the structure of the gut, the gut parameters are not recovered from these
subjects. We ran no replica for this model, so we cannot say how much of its non-identification belongs to the target
and how much to misfit.

Two further checks are in Appendix~\ref{app:results}. The gradient diagnostic ranks the three parameters as the profile
likelihood does (H6, planned with a post hoc synthetic clause, \resVerdictHSix) but orders the two timing rates
unstably across initializations (H10, planned, \resVerdictHTen). An external check against HbA1c shows the correlation
of an amortized estimate largely disappearing once fasting glucose is partialled out. A trivial proxy, the iAUC per
gram of logged carbohydrate, has a correlation of \resProxyRaw{} (\resProxyRawLow{} to \resProxyRawHigh) with HbA1c in
the same \resProxyN{} subjects, and \resProxyPartial{} (\resProxyPartialLow{} to \resProxyPartialHigh) with fasting
glucose partialled out, so the raw correlation of the amortized estimate, \resLeakRawSnpe, is not what the area alone
would give.
