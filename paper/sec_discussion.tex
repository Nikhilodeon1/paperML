\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What limits identification.}
Section~\ref{sec:theory} gives three reasons to expect more than we found.
The excursion centroid carries the first moment of the gut input, a gut model without the exchange symmetry has no mirror solution, and a known residue breaks the symmetry.
In our runs none of these delivered for most subjects.
The centroid bounded $\tau_1$ in a minority of subjects, the Dalla Man fit to the area bounded none of its gut rates, and a residue bounded the rates in only a few.
We read this as a shortage of information in the targets, with the symmetry as a secondary problem.
The centroid and residue runs used the coarser profile protocol, which limits how much weight their counts can bear.

\paragraph{What the replicas add.}
The positive result matters for the reading of the negative ones.
When the simulator generated the data and the carbohydrate values were exact, converged trace profiles for $\tau_1$ and $p$ were bounded for most random-truth subjects and covered at close to the nominal rate.
The method can therefore recover these combinations, so their loss under logging error cannot be blamed on a profile that never works.
That failure is a shift of the estimate with no matching change in width.
A likelihood that takes the logged load as exact has no way to express doubt about it.

\paragraph{Recommendations.}
For a twin of this kind fitted to free-living CGM data, our results support the following practice.
\begin{itemize}
\item Report $\ke$ and $\ka$ individually only with evidence beyond predictive accuracy. If timing is reported, report $\tau_1$, and describe it as a way to order subjects, not as a measured transit time. In our random-truth replicas the ordering degraded less than the coverage did; on real data neither can be checked.
\item Test the intervals on replicas that include a plausible input error, with truths spread through the search range as well as at the fitted values. In our runs, replicas with truths at the fitted values alone suggested that logging error closes intervals, and random truths showed that the direction depends on where the truth lies.
\item Do not use the number of bounded intervals to compare targets, solvers, or datasets as if more were better. Report coverage and interval width wherever the truth is known.
\item Score held-out targets that the fit did not use. In our replicas, the time of the glucose peak separated badly wrong rates from fitted ones in part, and the fitted target did not.
\end{itemize}
Models that treat the carbohydrate content as uncertain, such as measurement-error likelihoods or simulation-extrapolation, are natural next steps.
We did not test any of them.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{One model family with fixed constants.}
Every identifiability statement is conditional on the Bergman-type equations, the fixed constants ($\gamma$, $p_3$, $p_1$, $p_2$, $n$), a basal glucose of $90$\,mg\,dL$^{-1}$, and no sensor lag.
Body weight is each subject's recorded value.
Because $\gamma$ and $p_3$ enter only through their product with $\SI$, the fitted sensitivity is a lumped gain.

\paragraph{Assumed input error.}
The logging error is lognormal with unit mean, and its coefficient of variation is an assumption, not a measurement from these cohorts.
Real logging error may be biased or may depend on the meal.
We have not established why the estimates move toward slower absorption.

\paragraph{Replica design.}
The replica truths with fitted values come from one random seed, the random truths from two, and all of them reuse the \resCRandZeroTauN{} CGMacros meal schedules and noise estimates.
The simulated sensor noise is first-order autoregressive with parameters estimated from real residuals, which also absorb model misfit.
Each meal starts from an empty gut, although the median gap between logged meals is \resOverlapMedianGap\,h and \resOverlapWithinFour\% of gaps are shorter than $4$\,h; we did not simulate overlapping meals.

\paragraph{Structural misspecification.}
The second generator uses one distribution of Dalla Man parameters.
We did not fit a Dalla Man model to its own replica, add sensor lag or a free $p_2$ to the Bergman fit, or try a second generator distribution.
The Dalla Man control also ran under the coarser profile protocol, so its coverage figures are probably too low.

\paragraph{Search range and cohorts.}
The trace profiles search a rectangle in $(\log\tau_1,\log p)$ that contains rate pairs outside the primary box.
Profiles restricted to the feasible rates ran only under the coarser protocol (Appendix~\ref{apx:numerics}).
The cohorts are small.
Many Shanghai and standardized-meal estimates lie on a bound, Shanghai carbohydrates are estimated from free-text records, and CGMacros windows end at $180$\,min.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In a Bergman-type glucose twin fitted to postprandial CGM data, accurate prediction of the fitted area left the gut rates undetermined.
The full trace recovered two combinations of those rates in simulations where the model and its inputs were exact.
With carbohydrate logging error, the intervals stayed narrow while the estimates moved, and the number of bounded intervals could not be used to detect it.
Parameters from such a twin should therefore be reported with coverage checked under realistic input error, or reported as rankings at most.
Whether a likelihood that models the input error, or a meal protocol with designed contrasts, can restore calibrated timing estimates from free-living data is open.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}
The simulator studied here is a research model and is not validated for clinical use.
Our results point to a risk in deployment.
A twin that predicts a summary of the glucose response well can invite users or clinicians to read its parameters as physiological measurements, which could distort decisions about diet or treatment.
All analyses use public, de-identified datasets, and we collected no new data from people.
